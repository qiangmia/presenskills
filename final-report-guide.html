The one rule

Make a claim or tell a story for a specific room. The room should be able to retell it after you finish; if a listener can name your five moves, the presentation is ready.

02

Choose a purpose

Persuade. Inform. Entertain.

The course allows all three. Declare your purpose on the brief. The purpose shapes the content and the close; the delivery standard stays the same.

Persuade

Change a decision.

The audience should approve, fund, adopt, or stop something.

SCQA → executive flip → proposal → counterargument → implementation → CTA

Close with: the specific decision you want and by when.

Inform

Change understanding.

The audience should see a process, phenomenon, or idea clearly for the first time.

question → context → explanation → evidence → implications → so-what

Close with: what the audience should now notice or understand.

Entertain

Change attention or feeling.

The audience should be absorbed, amused, moved, or surprised — and remember why.

hook → setup → complication → turn → resolution → resonance

Close with: the feeling or insight the story leaves behind.

Purpose shapes structure

The same topic can be a policy pitch, an explainer, or a story. “AI policy” can be persuasive (adopt this policy), informative (how policies are being designed), or entertaining (a day in the life of an AI detector). Pick one purpose and commit to its structure.

Wildcard approval

Named topics are pre-approved; wildcards use this checklist.

  1. Declare the purpose: persuade, inform, or entertain.
  2. Connect it to campus life.
  3. Write the one-sentence takeaway.
  4. Map the five moves.
  5. Add an evidence plan or a performance plan.
  6. Add a visual plan and a Q&A plan.
  7. Assign group roles.
  8. Get the one-page brief approved before drafting.
Week 7 gives you a model opening. The cup case is a starting point; a group that chooses it builds the full essay, current evidence, the deck, and Q&A. A wildcard meets the same standard.

05

The build

From essay to deck.

Write the argument first; design the visual sequence second. A strong argument gives every slide its job.

01

Write the argument first.

Draft the essay or story before opening PowerPoint. A strong argument makes the slides easier to design.

Check · Can you state the claim in one sentence?
02

Find the one-sentence answer.

What should the audience think, feel, or do? This becomes the executive flip, the deck title, and the close.

Check · Is it specific enough to disagree with?
03

Map the five moves.

Hook, Bridge, Climax, Resolution, Takeaway. Put one sentence in each box before designing a single slide.

Check · Can a listener retell all five?
04

Choose evidence that can be seen.

One chart, map, photo, prototype, or demonstration per major claim. Keep only decoration that carries meaning.

Check · What does the audience look at, and what does it prove?
05

Build the visual argument.

One idea per slide, one first read, a clear title that states the claim, and enough whitespace to let it land.

Check · Can you read the point from the back row?
06

Rehearse aloud and cut.

Aim for a script that fits the time with pauses. Cut before you memorise.

Check · Does it fit the time limit with pauses?
The deck supports the script

The script carries the argument; the deck carries the evidence, the turn, and the visual anchor. Keep the slides visual and the argument spoken.

06

The shape

A visible narrative arc.

Every track uses the same five moves. SCQA is the persuasive micro-order; the arc is the macro shape. The room should be able to retell the moves from memory.

Hook
Bridge
Climax
Resolution
Takeaway
S · SituationThe shared ground and the minimum context. Usually the Bridge.
C · ComplicationThe change, problem, or tension. The Hook and/or Bridge.
Q · QuestionThe decision the complication forces. The Bridge or Climax.
A · AnswerThe governing thought. The Resolution and Takeaway.

The executive flip · persuasive only

“To cut campus cup waste by 80% within one semester, the university should replace the voluntary NT$5 mug discount with a mandatory NT$20 student-ID cup deposit.”

A → S → C. Lead with the answer for a time-poor room. For a story that depends on surprise, keep the answer for the ending.

Persuade“Approve the pilot by the end of this month.” A specific decision, owner, and date.
Inform“The next time you see the 5 p.m. crowd, you will know why it happens.” A new model the audience can use.
Entertain“You will see the convenience store differently after this.” A feeling or insight that stays.
The retell test

After a rehearsal, ask a listener to retell your talk in five sentences: Hook, Bridge, Climax, Resolution, Takeaway. When they can name all five, the arc is visible.

07

Design the deck

The six design levers.

Hierarchy, grid, space, balance, color, and contrast — the visual principles from Weeks 9–10. The audience scans a slide before it reads a word; these levers shape what the scan finds.

Hierarchy

One clear first read. The claim dominates; the title states the point.

Grid

A consistent set of columns and margins gives every element a home.

Space

Deliberate whitespace gives the eye room to rest; one idea per slide.

Balance

Visual weight distributed across the slide creates a calm, stable layout.

Color

A small, consistent palette uses color to mark meaning and grouping.

Contrast

Strong text/background contrast and emphasis through size and weight keep the slide readable from the back.

1 · Title / claim — the one sentence the room should remember.

2 · Hook — a photo, moment, number, or question.

3 · Context / stakes — the minimum the room needs.

4 · Evidence — one chart, map, or artifact that proves the point.

5 · Proposal / insight — the answer or the turn.

6 · Implementation / implications — who does what, or what changes.

7 · Counterargument / constraint — show you have stress-tested it.

8 · Close — CTA, so-what, or resonance.

Appendix — sources, backup charts, extra evidence for Q&A.

The back-row test

Project the deck and walk to the back of the room. Can you read the title? The body? The axis labels? Increase contrast or size, or cut content. The room is the real screen.

Q&A is part of the presentation

Pause two seconds. Answer Point first, then Reason and Example, then restate the Point. The Q&A lead decides who answers; the evidence lead supplies the number; the timekeeper keeps it under control.

09

Rehearse

Three rehearsals. Then the live presentation.

Rehearse aloud, with the deck, the timer, and someone watching. Three focused runs reveal the cuts, the handoffs, and the Q&A gaps.

Run 01

Table read

Read the full script aloud. Mark where the argument sags, where the handoffs are clumsy, and what can be cut. Start with the script alone.

Done when · the five moves are clear and the script is 10–15% shorter.
Run 02

Timed run

Add the deck, the clicker, the handoffs, and the timer. Mark every transition and every pause.

Done when · the group lands inside the limit with time for the close.
Run 03

Full dress

Project from the back row, test the room, record one run, and stress-test Q&A with the hardest questions you can imagine.

Done when · every member can adapt to a tech surprise and a tough question.

Record and review

  • Sound off: do the slides carry the argument on their own?
  • Screen off: does the spoken line work on its own?
  • Back row: can you read the title, body, and axis labels?
  • Handoffs: can you hear who speaks next before they speak?
  • Time: does the close get the last minute, or does it get rushed?

Use the instruments

Run the rehearsal with the Week 11 checklist. Open the live room for a peer audience and watch the domain averages. The goal is one clear target for the next run.

What counts, and who counts it.

Peer review · 10%

Peers rate the live delivery with the Week 11 checklist: 1–5 for each criterion, plus one specific note. The teacher uses the Full 18; peers can focus on the Core 13 if the room is large.

  1. Norm. Ten minutes before the session, rate a short sample together and agree what 1, 3, and 5 look like.
  2. Scan. Open the live room QR and start rating.
  3. Rate. Rate observable behaviour and specific choices.
  4. Aggregate. The peer score is the median of valid ratings, with at least five raters.
  5. Review. The teacher reads the distribution and notes; the median protects the grade from a single outlier.

Teamwork · 5%

Teamwork is the 5% process behind the script, deck, and delivery. It is assessed through how the group worked: roles, meetings, contribution, rehearsal, handoffs, and reliability.

A confidential peer evaluation is evidence. The teacher reviews it and may apply a contribution factor of 0.85–1.05 to an individual’s delivery and teamwork marks. The factor reflects documented contribution.

Talk early. Raise concerns early and log them with the group.

Keep the components distinct

Script judges the written argument. Deck judges the designed artifact. Delivery judges how both land in the room. Teamwork is the 5% process component, judged from how the group worked. The same feature can appear in several places, and it is judged differently each time.

What strong groups do

Write the argument before the slides.

Design the deck as visual evidence.

Deliver by paraphrasing and facing the room.

Coordinate as one group with clear handoffs.

Share Q&A across the team.

Use roles, logs, and rehearsals to make the process visible.

Questions

FAQ.

Can we choose the campus cup topic?

Yes. It is a valid option. Week 7 gives you the opening; add current evidence, a stakeholder angle, a full script, a deck, and Q&A. Build from the workshop paragraph as a model.

Can we do an informative or entertaining presentation?

Yes. Each purpose has a named topic and a wildcard. Declare the purpose in the brief and get approval for a wildcard before drafting. The campus-life connection and the five-move arc apply to every option.

Do we need the executive flip?

For a persuasive proposal, yes: lead with the answer, then compress the situation and complication. For informative, a BLUF is optional. For a story built on surprise, save the answer for the ending.

How many slides should we have?

Usually 8–12 content slides plus an appendix. One idea per slide, one clear first read. Follow the time limit announced in class; keep each slide to one point.

How is peer review counted?

Peer review is 10% of the total, inside the 60% delivery component. The teacher contributes 50%. Peer scores use the median of valid ratings, with at least five raters. The median protects the grade from a single outlier.

How do we handle uneven contribution?

Raise it early, in writing, and log it. The confidential peer evaluation and contribution matrix provide evidence for the teacher’s assessment. The teacher may apply a 0.85–1.05 factor to the individual’s delivery and teamwork marks.

Can we use AI?

Use AI transparently within course and department policy. Declare tool use, cite sources, verify every source and number, and be ready to defend the argument in Q&A.

How do we protect the time limit?

Protect the close and cut content elsewhere. Rehearse with the timer and a listener; the room’s patience is part of delivery, and a clear ending lands best.
